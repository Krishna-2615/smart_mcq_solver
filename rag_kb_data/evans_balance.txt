The Evans balance is a scientific instrument used to measure the magnetic susceptibility of materials. Invented by Dennis F. Evans in 1974, it offers a more compact, user-friendly, and cost-effective alternative to the traditional Gouy balance. While the Gouy balance measures the apparent change in mass of a sample when introduced to a static magnetic field, the Evans balance operates on an inverted principle. It uses a stationary sample and measures the force exerted on a suspended permanent magnet. The sample is placed in a glass tube and inserted between the poles of a strong pair of magnets suspended on a torsion mechanism. If the sample is paramagnetic, it pulls the magnets toward itself, causing a deflection. If diamagnetic, it repels the magnets. This deflection is detected optically, and a compensating electric current is applied to an opposing coil to bring the magnets back to their original position. The amount of current required is directly proportional to the magnetic susceptibility of the sample. This setup prevents the need for a massive electromagnet and a highly sensitive analytical balance, making it portable and ideal for routine laboratory use, particularly in inorganic chemistry for determining the number of unpaired electrons in transition metal complexes.
