In mathematics, logic, and formal theory, an axiom (or postulate) is a foundational statement, proposition, or premise that is accepted as true without requiring proof. Axioms serve as the absolute starting points from which all other theorems and truths within a specific logical system are logically derived. They are the structural bedrock of a formal theory. Unlike theorems, which must be rigorously proven using deductive reasoning, axioms are assumed to be self-evident or simply stipulated as the defined rules of the game. For example, in Euclidean geometry, an axiom is that a straight line segment can be drawn joining any two points. A formal theory is constructed by choosing a specific set of axioms and applying rules of inference to derive further conclusions. A well-constructed set of axioms should be consistent (they do not contradict each other) and independent (no axiom can be deduced from the others). In modern mathematics, axioms are not necessarily viewed as absolute, universal truths about physical reality, but rather as defining conditions for abstract mathematical structures. If you change the axioms, you create an entirely new mathematical universe, such as shifting from Euclidean geometry to non-Euclidean geometry by altering the parallel postulate.
