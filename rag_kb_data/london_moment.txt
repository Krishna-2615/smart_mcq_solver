The London moment is a fascinating quantum mechanical phenomenon observed in rotating superconductors. According to classical electromagnetism, a spinning charged object will generate a magnetic field. However, superconductors are electrically neutral overall, so classical physics predicts no significant magnetic field should arise simply from rotating one. Yet, when a superconductor is spun on its axis, it generates a weak, perfectly uniform magnetic field that points exactly along its axis of rotation. This field is called the London moment, named after the physicist Fritz London. The effect occurs because electrons in a superconductor form Cooper pairs and condense into a single macroscopic quantum state. When the bulk lattice of the superconductor rotates, the positively charged ions move with it. However, the superconducting electrons, governed by their collective quantum wave function, lag slightly behind due to their inertia. This minuscule difference in velocity between the rotating positive ions and the lagging negative electrons creates a net internal electrical current, which in turn produces the magnetic field. The strength of the London moment is strictly proportional to the rotation speed. This phenomenon has highly practical applications; it was famously utilized in the Gravity Probe B satellite, where incredibly precise superconducting gyroscopes used the London moment to track their spin axes and test the frame-dragging predictions of Einstein's General Relativity.
