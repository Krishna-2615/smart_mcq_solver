A regular polytope is the generalization of highly symmetric geometric shapes across any number of dimensions. In two dimensions, these are regular polygons, such as equilateral triangles and squares, where all sides and internal angles are identical. In three dimensions, they are regular polyhedra, also known as the Platonic solids: the tetrahedron, cube, octahedron, dodecahedron, and icosahedron. In these 3D shapes, all faces are identical regular polygons, and the same number of faces meet at every vertex. The concept of a regular polytope extends this strict symmetry into four dimensions and beyond. A four-dimensional regular polytope has three-dimensional regular polyhedra as its 'cells.' Interestingly, the number of possible regular polytopes varies dramatically depending on the dimension. While there are infinitely many regular polygons in 2D, and exactly five regular polyhedra in 3D, four-dimensional space allows for six regular polytopes (including the tesseract, or 4D cube). In five dimensions and all higher dimensions, the geometric constraints become so tight that there are only exactly three regular polytopes possible: the simplex (the higher-dimensional analog of a triangle/tetrahedron), the hypercube, and the orthoplex (the higher-dimensional analog of an octahedron). Regular polytopes represent the pinnacle of geometric symmetry and are heavily studied in group theory and algebraic topology.
