The redshift-distance relationship is one of the most profound observational pillars of modern cosmology, providing the primary evidence for the expanding universe. First established by Edwin Hubble in the 1920s, the relationship demonstrates a direct correlation between a galaxy's distance from Earth and its cosmological redshift—the degree to which its light is stretched toward longer, redder wavelengths. The underlying cause of this relationship is not that galaxies are moving through space, but rather that the fabric of space itself is expanding. As light travels across this expanding space, its wavelength is stretched proportionally. Because light from more distant galaxies has been traveling for a longer period, it has been subjected to more cosmic expansion, resulting in a higher redshift. This linear relationship is mathematically expressed as Hubble's Law. By accurately measuring the redshift of a distant galaxy via its emission or absorption spectra, astronomers can deduce its recessional velocity and use the Hubble constant to calculate its distance. This allows cosmologists to map the three-dimensional structure of the universe. Furthermore, studying the redshift-distance relationship at immense distances—specifically by observing Type Ia supernovae—revealed that the expansion of the universe is not slowing down, but accelerating, leading to the discovery of Dark Energy. Thus, this fundamental relationship allows us to determine the expansion history, age, and ultimate fate of the universe.
