Redshift is a fundamental astronomical concept describing the phenomenon where electromagnetic radiation from an object undergoes an increase in wavelength, shifting toward the red end of the spectrum. This shift occurs due to the Doppler effect when a light source moves away from an observer, much like the pitch of a siren drops as a fire engine speeds away. In cosmology, the most significant form is the cosmological redshift, which is caused by the metric expansion of space itself. As the universe expands, the space between galaxies stretches, lengthening the wavelength of light traveling through it. This relationship is codified in Hubble's Law, which states that the recessional velocity of a galaxy is proportional to its distance from Earth. Consequently, redshift serves as a crucial cosmic yardstick. By measuring the redshift of a galaxy, astronomers can determine how fast it is moving away and, by extension, calculate its distance and the time at which the light was emitted. High-redshift objects, such as galaxies observed by the James Webb Space Telescope (like CEERS-93316), represent the early universe, allowing scientists to look billions of years into the past, mere millions of years after the Big Bang. Additionally, gravitational redshift occurs when light climbs out of a deep gravitational well, a key prediction of Einstein's General Relativity. Ultimately, redshift provides the foundational evidence for an expanding universe and remains essential for mapping the cosmos.
