Maxwell's Demon is a famous thought experiment proposed by the Scottish physicist James Clerk Maxwell in 1867 to question the absolute validity of the Second Law of Thermodynamics. The Second Law states that the total entropy (disorder) of an isolated system must never decrease over time. Maxwell imagined a sealed container divided into two chambers (A and B) by a wall with a microscopic door. A hypothetical, microscopic intelligent being—the 'demon'—guards this door. The container is filled with a gas at uniform temperature, meaning the gas molecules are moving at various random speeds. The demon watches the molecules approaching the door. It opens the door to allow fast-moving (hotter) molecules to pass from chamber A to B, and slow-moving (colder) molecules to pass from B to A. Over time, chamber B becomes significantly hotter, and chamber A becomes colder, seemingly decreasing the total entropy of the system without doing any physical work, thus violating the Second Law. For over a century, this paradox puzzled physicists. It was eventually resolved by integrating information theory with thermodynamics. Physicists like Rolf Landauer and Charles Bennett demonstrated that the demon must store information about the molecules' speeds. Erasing this information from the demon's memory requires energy and generates heat, increasing the universe's entropy by an amount greater than the entropy decreased by sorting the gas.
