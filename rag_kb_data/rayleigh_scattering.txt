Rayleigh scattering is the physical phenomenon responsible for the colors we observe in the sky, including the blue of the daytime sky and the yellowish-red hues of the sun at sunrise and sunset. Named after the British physicist Lord Rayleigh, who first mathematically described it, this type of scattering occurs when light interacts with particles or molecules that are much smaller than its wavelength. In the Earth's atmosphere, sunlight collides with nitrogen and oxygen molecules. Sunlight is composed of all the colors of the visible spectrum, each with a different wavelength. Rayleigh scattering is highly wavelength-dependent; shorter wavelengths, such as blue and violet, are scattered much more efficiently than longer wavelengths, like red and yellow. During the day, when the sun is high in the sky, this intense scattering of short wavelengths causes the sky to appear blue to our eyes (our eyes are less sensitive to violet). However, when we look directly toward the sun, especially when it is lower on the horizon, the sunlight must pass through a much thicker layer of the atmosphere. By the time the light reaches our eyes, most of the blue and violet light has been scattered away in other directions. The longer wavelengths—yellow, orange, and red—are largely unaffected and pass straight through, which is why the sun appears slightly yellowish during the day and takes on deep red colors at sunset.
