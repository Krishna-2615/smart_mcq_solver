Lorentz symmetry, or Lorentz invariance, is a foundational principle in modern physics, particularly within Albert Einstein's theory of Special Relativity. It states that the laws of physics are the same for all observers moving at a constant velocity relative to one another, regardless of their orientation or the speed at which they are traveling (as long as it is not accelerating). This symmetry implies that the fundamental equations describing nature—such as Maxwell's equations for electromagnetism and the Dirac equation for quantum mechanics—do not change their mathematical form under Lorentz transformations. These transformations account for how space and time coordinates change between different inertial reference frames, factoring in relativistic effects like time dilation and length contraction as velocities approach the speed of light. Because of Lorentz symmetry, there is no 'absolute' state of rest in the universe; motion is entirely relative. It mandates that the speed of light in a vacuum is a universal constant, identical for all observers. Lorentz invariance is deeply embedded in the Standard Model of particle physics and General Relativity. Testing the limits of Lorentz symmetry remains an active area of research, as any violation would strongly indicate new physics beyond current theoretical frameworks, potentially pointing toward quantum gravity or string theory.
