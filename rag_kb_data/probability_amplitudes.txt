In quantum mechanics, a probability amplitude is a complex number used to describe the fundamental behavior and state of a quantum system, such as a particle. Unlike classical physics, where objects have definite properties (like an exact position or momentum), quantum systems exist in a state of superposition, represented mathematically by a wave function. This wave function is made up of probability amplitudes. The most crucial feature of a probability amplitude is that it does not represent a physical probability itself; rather, its squared magnitude yields the actual probability of a specific outcome occurring when a measurement is made. This principle is known as the Born rule, named after physicist Max Born. Because probability amplitudes are complex numbers, they possess both a magnitude and a phase. This phase is critical because it allows probability amplitudes to interfere with one another, just like ripples on a pond. When calculating the probability of a particle traveling from point A to point B, one must sum the probability amplitudes of all possible paths. If the phases align, they undergo constructive interference, increasing the likelihood of that outcome. If they are out of phase, they undergo destructive interference, decreasing or completely canceling out the probability. This interference of probability amplitudes is the mathematical mechanism behind quintessential quantum phenomena like the double-slit experiment.
